Abstract

The FAIR Data Train Visit Protocol specifies the conversation between a Train Handler and a Data Station for one visit — one train, at one station, under one agreement. It covers how a visit is dispatched (by push, or by a station that accepts no inbound connection polling for it), how the station checks what arrived, how an agreement is negotiated on arrival and signed by both parties before anything runs, how every decision the station takes is reported as an event with a justification, how a result is inspected before it leaves and how state is handed from one visit to the next by location and digest and never by value. It defines the three message types — the visit descriptor, the visit event and the visit-result envelope — the two HTTP interfaces, the outcome vocabulary a completeness statement reports to a data consumer verbatim, and what a conforming station and a conforming Handler must do.

1. Introduction

This section is non-normative.

In the FAIR Data Train the computation travels to the data. A train — a query, an API call, a script or a container — is sent to a Data Station that hosts the data, runs there under terms negotiated on arrival, and only what passes the station’s inspection leaves. The Train Handler is the consumer’s agent that sends the train and collects what comes back; the station is where the data controller’s conditions are enforced.

The Visit Protocol is the data plane of the FDT ecosystem: the conversation between a Handler and a station for one visit — one train, at one station, under one agreement (ADR-014, ADR-027 [FDT-ADR]). Every multi-station itinerary a Handler can run — a single visit, fan-out, a sequence, a discovery chain, iterative rounds, two-phase selection, composition — decomposes into visits, which is why this is deliberately the only interface a station has to implement to take part in any of them. The Handler owns the run; the station owns the visit.

The protocol is defined by its artefacts as much as by this text. Its three message types are JSON Schemas, its two interfaces are OpenAPI documents, its RDF payloads are validated by SHACL shapes, and the examples in this document are the published fixtures every implementation is tested against — not documents typed into prose. Appendix B: Normative artefacts names them, pinned to a commit.

1.1. Scope

This section is non-normative.

This specification covers, for one visit: dispatch in two directions (§ 6 Dispatch); what a station checks when a train arrives; negotiation on arrival, the derivation of the agreement and the signatures of both parties (§ 7 Negotiation on arrival); execution and the inspection of the result (§ 8 Execution and inspection); the hand-over of state between visits (§ 9 Onward state and hand-over); how every decision is reported (§ 10 Reporting); how a visit ends (§ 11 Outcomes, cancellation, retries, revocation); the messages (§ 12 Messages) and the interfaces (§ 13 Station API, § 14 Handler API — the station-facing surface); and the security model (§ 15 Security).

Three things are not in this protocol and live in their own contracts: the station’s metadata and catalogue ([FDT-ARCHITECTURE] §3.1.1, built on [FDP] and [VOCAB-DCAT-3]); the controller’s actions over their own data — authoring conditions, approving, revoking, suspending —​which reach a station through its console or an Individual Gateway; and the catalogues of the registry and the Train Depot, from which a train is dereferenced by IRI.

1.2. Reading this document

This section is non-normative.

Decisions behind the protocol are recorded as Architecture Decision Records [FDT-ADR] and are cited as ADR-nnn where the reasoning may matter to an implementer. Questions the protocol leaves open are listed in § 17 Open points with their register identifiers (Qnn); each has a stated default that implementations follow until it is decided.

Member names in message tables are written as camelCase JSON keys. Where a value is an IRI, it is a string in the JSON and an IRI in the vocabulary the schema maps it to.

2. Conformance classes

Three conformance classes are defined. The requirements are stated in the body of this document with the key words of [RFC2119] and collected here.

Push-dispatch station

A Data Station that accepts inbound connections. It MUST implement the Station API (§ 13 Station API) — at least /, POST /visits, GET /visits/{visitId}, GET /visits/{visitId}/events, GET /visits/{visitId}/result, POST /visits/{visitId}/cancel and POST /visits/{visitId}/agreement/signature; MUST validate descriptors and embedded ODRL requests against the schemas and shapes of Appendix B: Normative artefacts; MUST verify the payload digest against the train’s depot offer before anything runs; MUST emit every decision as an event with a justification; MUST sign the assigner’s side of every agreement it concludes and MUST run nothing under an agreement that does not carry the assignee’s signature; MUST NOT let record-level content into an envelope; and, when it receives a hand-over, MUST check the lineage rule of § 9.2 What arrives before fetching anything.

Poll-dispatch station

A Data Station that accepts no inbound connection. It MUST implement the poll half of the Handler API as a client (§ 14 Handler API — the station-facing surface) — GET /stations/{stationId}/pending-visits, POST /visits/{visitId}/ack, POST /visits/{visitId}/events, PUT /visits/{visitId}/result and POST /visits/{visitId}/agreement — and otherwise meets every requirement of a push-dispatch station. It MUST report by callback, that being the only direction it can open.

Train Handler (conformance class)

A Handler MUST mint the visit IRI of every visit and use it as the idempotency key; MUST embed an ODRL request valid against fdts:RequestShape or an existing agreement IRI; MUST honour the plan’s approvalWait and failure policy; MUST verify the assigner’s signature over the stated digest before signing the assignee’s side, and MUST refuse to sign a document the station never signed; MUST de-duplicate events on id and order them on sequence; MUST name handOver.fromVisit in the same descriptor’s precededBy whenever it carries a hand-over; MUST assemble the run record and completeness statement from envelopes and events only; and MUST hold non-personal, aggregate results only (ADR-015).

3. Terminology

run

One execution of a plan — a train, its parameter values, an itinerary and a request template — by a Handler. A run is decomposed into visits.

hop, phase

A visit’s position in its itinerary: the hop counts outward from the run’s entry stations; the phase is the order of the itinerary phase it belongs to. Both are integers from 1.

attempt

A retry re-dispatches the same visit IRI with attempt incremented, under the same agreement. Attempts of one visit share its identity and its agreement.

agreement

The odrl:Agreement [ODRL-MODEL] a station derives from the controller’s offer and the train’s request for one visit, pinned by digest and signed by both parties. One per visit (ADR-014).

checkpoint

One of the points at which a station decides: PEP 1 (arrival), NEG (negotiation), PEP 2 (before execution), EXEC (execution), PEP 3 (result inspection) and OBL (post-execution obligations).

dispatch mode

How a visit reaches a station: push — the Handler posts it — or poll — the station fetches it from the Handler (ADR-004).

reporting mode

How a station’s events and envelope reach the Handler: callback — the station posts them to a per-visit URL — or poll — the Handler reads them from the station. Note that the two senses of poll are different parties reading in different directions; § 7.4 Signatures of both parties depends on keeping them apart.

onward state

What leaves a station other than the result delivered to the Handler: state of class Aggregate or Pseudonym carried to another station, directly in a choreographed train or through a linkage station. RecordLevel never leaves a station.

hand-over

State produced by an earlier visit of the same run and named, by location and digest, as the input of a later one.

outcome

The one word a visit ends in: Delivered, Refused, Rejected, TimedOut, Failed, Revoked, or — recorded by the Handler alone — Skipped (§ 11 Outcomes, cancellation, retries, revocation).

canonical form

Of a graph G: its [RDF-CANON] (URDNA2015) canonical form, serialised as N-Quads, the lines sorted, joined with \n, with a trailing \n, encoded UTF-8. Every digest a party signs or pins is "sha256:" followed by the lowercase hex SHA-256 of a canonical form.

A station MUST treat a repeated POST /visits with the same visit and attempt as a replay and answer with the visit’s current status. A station advertises that it speaks this protocol in its self-description: its dispatch endpoint is a dcat:DataService whose dct:conformsTo is the protocol identifier https://w3id.org/fdt/visit-protocol/v1, and whose dcat:endpointURL is the base URL of the Station API.

5. Overview of a visit

This section is non-normative; the sections it points at are normative.

  1. Dispatch. The Handler composes a visit descriptor and pushes it to the station, or a poll-dispatch station fetches it from the Handler (§ 6 Dispatch).

  2. PEP 1. The station verifies the caller’s credential against the identity providers it trusts for the visit’s network, validates the descriptor against its schema and the embedded ODRL request against fdts:RequestShape, checks that the train’s payload digest equals the one in the train’s depot offer, and checks that it supports the train’s mechanism. A failure rejects the visit; otherwise it emits visit.received.

  3. Negotiation. The station matches the request against the controller’s offer for the visit’s network and the owner’s policy. The outcome is a match, a case put to a person, or a refusal — which is the controller’s decision, never the station’s (§ 7 Negotiation on arrival).

  4. Signatures. The station derives the agreement, signs the assigner’s side and asks for the assignee’s; the Handler verifies the terms and that signature and signs the assignee’s side with the train owner’s key. Only then does the agreement become active, and only then does anything run (§ 7.4 Signatures of both parties).

  5. PEP 2 and execution. The station checks that the agreement is active and about this visit and that its quotas allow the run, queues it, and runs the payload through the adapter for the train’s mechanism.

  6. PEP 3. The station validates the result against the train’s declared output schema, checks every duty of the agreement on it, and decides what leaves and where (§ 8 Execution and inspection). A failure rejects the result; nothing leaves.

  7. Delivery. The visit-result envelope is pushed to the Handler’s callback URL or fetched by the Handler, and the visit ends in one of the outcomes of § 11 Outcomes, cancellation, retries, revocation.

6. Dispatch

6.1. Push dispatch

Under push dispatch the Handler POSTs the visit descriptor to the station’s dispatch endpoint, read from the station’s self-description. The station MUST answer 202 Accepted with a Location header naming the visit’s status URL and a VisitStatus body when the visit was taken; 200 with the current status on an idempotent replay; 400 with a Problem when the visit is rejected at PEP 1; 401 when the credential is missing or untrusted; 403 with a Problem when the visit is refused at negotiation; 409 when the same visit IRI is known with a different, still-running attempt; and 415 when the station does not support the train’s mechanism.

6.2. Poll dispatch

A station that cannot accept inbound connections MUST instead call GET /stations/{stationId}/pending-visits on the Handler, which returns the descriptors waiting for it and MAY long-poll for up to wait seconds. For each descriptor it takes, the station MUST POST /visits/{visitId}/ack with its first event — visit.received — or with a Problem if the visit is rejected at PEP 1. An acknowledgement answered 409 means the Handler withdrew the visit meanwhile, and the station MUST NOT start it. Beyond the gateway, processing is identical to push dispatch (ADR-004).

A station advertises its dispatch mode or modes in its self-description; the Handler chooses per station.

6.3. Reporting modes

The descriptor’s reporting.mode selects how the station’s events and envelope reach the Handler. Under callback the station POSTs events to {callbackUrl}/events and PUTs the envelope to {callbackUrl}/result, presenting the descriptor’s callbackToken as a bearer token. Under poll the Handler reads GET /visits/{visitId}/events?after=n — or subscribes with Accept: text/event-stream — and fetches GET /visits/{visitId}/result. A poll-dispatch station MUST report by callback.

6.4. Idempotency, attempts and withdrawal

A Handler retrying a Failed visit MUST re-dispatch the same visit IRI with attempt incremented by one and negotiation.agreement set to the existing agreement; the station MUST reuse that agreement rather than negotiate anew. The Handler withdraws a visit with POST /visits/{visitId}/cancel, stating a reason and a cause from approval-timeout, fail-run, stopped-by-person, deadline or replanned; a queued or pending visit ends at once and a running one stops at its next checkpoint (§ 11.1 Cancellation).

7. Negotiation on arrival

7.1. Request or agreement

The descriptor’s negotiation member carries either an odrl:Request — JSON-LD, validated at PEP 1 against fdts:RequestShape — or the IRI of an existing active agreement (rounds of an iterative pattern, retries). With an agreement IRI the station MUST verify that the agreement is active, that its target and assignee match the visit, and that it was concluded at this station; otherwise the visit is Rejected at PEP 2.

With a request, the station MUST match it against the controller’s offers for the visit’s network (ADR-017) and the station owner’s policy, and MUST emit negotiation.requested first. The request names the network under fdt-p:underNetwork, the train under fdt-p:train, the target dataset or part, the assignee and the actions it asks for with their constraints, of which a purpose is required. The descriptor’s consumer.party and the request’s odrl:assignee MUST name the same party; a station MUST reject at PEP 1 a descriptor in which they differ, since the agreement binds the request’s assignee while every record a controller is shown names the descriptor’s consumer.

7.2. Matched, pending, refused

Matching has three outcomes.

Matched

The offer and the request are satisfiable and the station may conclude. It emits negotiation.matched with a justification and proceeds to derive and sign the agreement. negotiation.matched MUST be emitted only when the two are satisfiable: the state means exactly that, and maps to the Dataspace Protocol’s OFFERED or AGREED, neither of which describes a request nothing can satisfy.

Pending approval

A person must decide. A station emits negotiation.pending-approval carrying an approval object that names the controller who must decide, the mode that says why a person is deciding, the rule that imposed it, the station’s recommendation, the eligibility facts it could not establish, and its own expiresAt. The modes are HumanRequiredByRegulation (the network’s regime bars automated decisions), HumanByControllerPreference (the offer asks for a person), HumanByStationPolicy (the operator configured one) and HumanByInconclusiveEvaluation (the controller’s conditions did not answer — the request asks for something no permission addresses, or a fact could not be established; ADR-050). Where a machine may not grant, it may not refuse either (ADR-032): a visit whose evaluation recommends refusal in a network that bars automated decisions goes from negotiation.requested straight to negotiation.pending-approval, without a negotiation.matched, and the recommendation is what tells the two apart. A recommendation is not an outcome.

Refused

The controller’s standing conditions declined the request — conclusively, under a rule of the offer — or a person did. The station emits negotiation.refused with the stated reason and the justification, and the visit’s outcome is Refused. This is a governance decision by a named party and is never reported in any other word (§ 11 Outcomes, cancellation, retries, revocation).

While a visit is pending, the Handler MUST wait up to the plan’s approvalWait and then either withdraw the visit (POST /visits/{visitId}/cancel, cause approval-timeout) or keep waiting as its failure policy says. The station MAY apply its own timeout (approval.expiresAt). A visit withdrawn because nobody decided ends TimedOut, and MUST NOT be reported as Refused or Rejected: nobody decided anything, and that is the fact to report.

7.3. Derivation of the agreement

An agreement that merely has the right shape is not an agreement anyone can audit. A station MUST derive the agreement from the offer O (restricted to the visit’s network), the request R and the visit V by the following rule, which fdt-commons executes over every published agreement and which a station’s own agreements are held to:

  1. Actions. The agreement grants exactly actions(R) ∩ actions(O). If no action survives there is no agreement and the outcome is Refused, naming the rule.

  2. Constraints. Every odrl:LeftOperand of the FDT profile has a fdt-p:constraintRole: Eligibility constraints (legal basis, consumer type, network, jurisdiction, train, mechanism) are established once at negotiation and become evidence, never terms; Usage constraints (purpose, onward state class, k-anonymity, retention, validity window) become terms re-checked at PEP 2 and PEP 3. For each granted action and each usage operand O's matching rule constrains, the agreement carries what was asked, bounded by what was offered: R's constraint if O admits it, otherwise O's. Every usage constraint of O's matching rule MUST appear, and every usage constraint R placed on itself MUST appear too.

  3. Prohibitions and duties of O are carried verbatim. Nothing in negotiation may drop or weaken one, and no approver may waive one.

  4. Evidence. For every eligibility constraint of O the agreement carries one fdt-p:evidence node: the fact, the established value, who attested it and when. The person who dispatched the train appears as a pseudonym the station can resolve in its own audit log; their identifier does not enter the agreement.

  5. Pinning. The agreement carries fdt-p:derivedFromOffer, fdt-p:derivedFromRequest, fdt-p:train, and the digests fdt-p:offerDigest, fdt-p:requestDigest and fdt-p:payloadDigest of each as it stood.

  6. Parties. The agreement’s odrl:assigner MUST be the offer’s assigner and its odrl:assignee MUST be the request’s assignee; odrl:target and fdt-p:underNetwork MUST be the visit’s; dspace:timestamp and fdt-p:authorisationMode MUST be present.

A station policy may narrow the result further; narrowing is always permitted and widening never is.

7.4. Signatures of both parties

The station writes the agreement and is a party to it; digests make a rewrite detectable by whoever kept the original, but prove nothing to a third party who kept nothing — a supervisory authority, or a controller asking later under what terms their data was touched. So each party signs (ADR-038), and an agreement carries one fdt-p:signature of each role, fdt-p:AssignerSignature and fdt-p:AssigneeSignature.

7.4.1. What is signed

The signed digest is the SHA-256 of the canonical form of the agreement’s concise bounded description **with every fdt-p:signature node excluded** — not only the signer’s own — so that both parties sign one string, neither signature covers the other, the order they were made in is not part of what was agreed, and neither party can invalidate the other’s by re-signing.

A signature is Ed25519 [RFC8032] (EdDSA) over the digest string as UTF-8, encoded base64url without padding. fdt-p:keyId is the IRI of the public key in the signer’s own published key set [RFC7517], so a verifier needs nothing from either party. Signing keys are not the bearer tokens of § 15 Security: a token proves who called, a signature proves who agreed.

7.4.2. Who signed, and for whom

fdt-p:signedBy names whose key it is. fdt-p:onBehalfOf names whose agreement it is, and MUST be present exactly when the two differ — when an agent signed.

signedBy onBehalfOf
The train owner signs (assignee) the owner; the key is published by their Handler absent
A data controller with their own key signs (assigner) the controller absent
The station signs for a controller who holds no key the station the controller

The controller signed and the station signed for the controller are different facts with different weight, and a record that let them be confused would be worth less than no signature, because a verifier would read the weaker as the stronger. A station signing as an agent MUST carry onBehalfOf; a party signing for themselves MUST NOT; a signature naming as its principal the party who made it MUST be refused — a party is not their own agent.

7.4.3. The exchange, in two shapes

The two-signature rule fixes the order: the station derives the terms and signs the assigner’s side; the Handler verifies the terms and that signature and signs the assignee’s side; the station stores the fully signed agreement and only then emits negotiation.active. Which party opens the call is decided by the dispatch mode and by nothing else.

Push-dispatched station. After negotiation.matched the station emits negotiation.awaiting-signature, carrying the agreement IRI and, in the justification’s evidence, agreementDigest — the digest to sign. The Handler fetches the agreement (GET /agreements/{agreementId}), computes the digest for itself from the document it read, checks that it equals the one stated, verifies the assigner’s signature over it, checks the terms, signs the digest with the train owner’s key and POSTs an AgreementSignature to POST /visits/{visitId}/agreement/signature. The station verifies it against the key it holds for that party, stores it, emits negotiation.active and queues the run. The visit stays at Checking throughout: nothing about it is waiting on a person.

Poll-dispatched station. The station has no inbound port, so it cannot be told to wait to be called; it emits no negotiation.awaiting-signature and instead opens one round trip of its own: POST /visits/{visitId}/agreement on the Handler, with a CountersignatureRequest carrying the agreement IRI, the agreement itself — as canonical N-Quads or Turtle, never a JSON projection, because the document is what the signature is checked against — and the assigner’s signature. The Handler applies exactly the checks above and answers 201 with the assignee’s Countersignature, or 422 with a CountersignatureRefused. The station then stores both signatures, emits negotiation.active and queues the run. Because no wait occurred, the stream MUST NOT contain negotiation.awaiting-signature; instead negotiation.active carries signatureRoute: station-initiated and dispatchMode: poll in its justification’s evidence, so which way the exchange went is stated rather than inferred from an absence.

In both shapes the Handler MUST NOT sign a document the station has not signed, MUST NOT sign a digest other than the one it computed from the document itself, and MUST NOT sign a document whose assigner signature is for a party other than the agreement’s assigner.

7.4.4. Refusing a signature

A verifier refusing a signature MUST say which of four things it found, because a verifier has to distinguish them and one status for all four would leave the first hunting a forgery that never happened:

Reason Meaning
UnknownKey The verifier holds no key for the signing party. A key-distribution problem; says nothing about the signature.
DoesNotVerify The signature is wrong.
WrongParty Valid cryptography by somebody who is not the party in that role, nor an agent naming them.
WrongDigest The digest the signature states is not the canonical digest of the document in hand: the two sides are not looking at the same bytes.

Keys are configured out of band, symmetrically: a station holds the public key of each train owner whose Handler may visit it; a Handler holds the public key of each station it dispatches to. A verifier MUST NOT take the key from the document it is verifying, which would establish only that the signer holds the key they nominated. A party for which no key is held is answered UnknownKey. Whether a network’s trust anchors may vouch for a station’s signing key is open (Q61).

7.4.5. When the assignee does not sign

An agreement only one party has signed is not an agreement, and nothing runs under one. If the assignee’s Handler cannot be reached or will not accept the terms, the visit does not proceed. The station records no outcome and the visit rests at negotiation.awaiting-signature until it is withdrawn, which is the behaviour a pending approval already has; the Handler reports its own run as failed when it stops waiting. What outcome the station should record is open (Q27). It is deliberately not Refused: a consumer walking away is not the controller refusing.

8. Execution and inspection

PEP 2. Before execution the station MUST check that the agreement is active and carries both signatures, that it is about this visit — its target, train, network and assignee match the descriptor’s — and that its usage terms admit the run now (the validity window) and that the station’s quotas allow it. A failure here rejects the visit at PEP 2, and the station MUST emit visit.queued on success.

EXEC. The adapter for train.mechanism executes the payload with the descriptor’s parameters, passed to the adapter unchanged; the station emits visit.running and MAY emit visit.progress. An error in the adapter or the infrastructure ends the visit Failed.

PEP 3. A train has run and whatever it produced is inside the station. Everything before this point decided whether the train could run; this decides whether what it produced may leave, and the first cannot answer the second: the same train under the same agreement produces a releasable result for one parameter and an unreleasable one for another. The station MUST perform these checks in order, and the first that fails ends the inspection with nothing released:

  1. The train declares an output schema (fdt-o:generatesOutput → dct:conformsTo, a JSON Schema [JSON-SCHEMA]) and it resolves.

  2. That schema is closed: every object sets additionalProperties: false and every array describes its items. Against an open schema, a result carrying an identifier on every row validates, and "the result conforms to the declared output" would be a statement about nothing.

  3. The result validates against it, with no relaxation.

  4. The onward state class being released is admitted by the agreement’s fdt-p:deliverResult permission and by the descriptor’s onward.allowedClasses. RecordLevel never leaves a station, whatever any agreement says.

  5. Every duty of the agreement holds on the result. A k-anonymity duty is applied to every value the declared output schema marks x-fdt-subjectCount: true; a count of 0 does not violate it and a count between 1 and k−1 does. A duty the station cannot check is not a duty that passes: if the agreement carries a k duty and the schema marks no subject count, the result is rejected with that stated. Prohibitions on what the consumer does after delivery (fdt-p:reidentify, odrl:sell) cannot be checked by inspecting a result; they bind the consumer through the agreement and the station MUST NOT list them as passed.

  6. The envelope names the schema it was checked against (inspection.outputSchema), so an auditor can re-run steps 2 and 3 without the station’s cooperation.

The inspection’s outcome is passed, redacted (cells suppressed to meet a threshold — a Delivered visit) or rejected (a Rejected visit, releasing nothing — not a partial result, not a count beyond what the justification states). The station emits visit.inspecting and then visit.delivered or visit.rejected at checkpoint PEP3, with the verdict’s evidence.

9. Onward state and hand-over

9.1. What leaves

The descriptor’s onward block is the plan’s request; PEP 3 applies the controller’s offer, which may be stricter. The destination is handler (the default: the envelope’s result and artifacts), linkage (Pseudonym-class state is handed to the named linkage station under the named join key; the envelope records the hand-over digest and the linkage station verifies it on its own visit) or train (a choreographed script or container train carries state and a routing instruction; in version 1 the train returns to the Handler between hops, so the envelope’s routing.nextStations is what the Handler dispatches next, inspected at PEP 3 like any other output). Query and API trains MUST NOT use destination train.

9.2. What arrives

A later visit of the same run receives earlier state through the descriptor’s handOver block, which names the hand-over and never carries it: fromVisit, fromStation, url, mediaType, digest, stateClass, and optionally joinKey, conformsTo, duties and bytes. The Handler holds a name, which is what ADR-015 permits it to hold; the bytes stay at the station that produced them. The following rules apply:

  • The object is closed and has no member that takes content. url MUST be an https location: data: and blob:, the two ways a reference carries its own payload, are refused by the schema rather than deprecated in prose, and so is plaintext http, because all traffic between the parties is over TLS. The same pattern binds artifacts[].url in the envelope that feeds this block (§ 12.3 Visit-result envelope).

  • **handOver.fromVisit MUST also appear in the descriptor’s precededBy.** The hand-over is the reason this visit follows that one, and a descriptor naming a predecessor in one place that its own lineage does not name in the other describes two different runs. JSON Schema cannot compare a member with the contents of another member’s array, so the schema requires only that a hand-over is not carried without a lineage; the membership is a rule of this protocol, binding on the Handler that composes the descriptor, and a receiving station MUST check it before fetching anything — it is decidable from the one document in front of it at PEP 1.

  • A descriptor that carries a hand-over MUST ask permission to accept it: its request MUST contain a permission for the action fdt-p:acceptHandOver, constrained by fdt-p:inboundStateClass to say which class of state it accepts. Being asked to join one’s own records against a list a third party composed is a different thing to consent to from being asked to run a container.

  • The receiving station fetches url, hashes what it got and compares it to digest — the same value the producing station reported as onward.handedOverDigest — so the hand-over is bound to bytes on both sides of the hop.

Whether a station that cannot verify the bytes must refuse at PEP 1 or may run and report the result unverifiable (Q133, Q130), who may fetch a hand-over from the station holding it and under which credential (Q136), and how a hop with two inputs is expressed (Q135) are open; version 1 names the location and nothing else.

10. Reporting

10.1. Events

Every decision a station takes and every state change of a visit MUST be emitted as a visit event (ADR-027). The ordered event stream is the source of truth for the Handler’s view of the run and for the audit trail a controller reads; the Handler needs nothing else to drive its itinerary map and its completeness statement. Delivery is at least once: a Handler MUST de-duplicate on id and order on sequence. A station MAY batch events in one callback if all belong to the visit in the path.

Under reporting mode poll, GET /visits/{visitId}/events?after=n returns the events with sequence greater than n; with Accept: text/event-stream a station MAY stream new events as Server-Sent Events, each data: line one event object and id: the sequence.

Every decision event carries a justification: the decision, the rule that fired, the offer, agreement or station policy it came from, and evidence — counts, digests, cell counts, never record-level content.

10.2. Result and artefacts

The visit-result envelope is available once the visit is in a terminal state: under callback the station PUTs it to {callbackUrl}/result; under poll the Handler fetches GET /visits/{visitId}/result, which answers 409 while the visit is not yet terminal. For a non-success outcome the envelope is returned with an empty result, the outcome in state and the stated reason. Artefacts too large to inline are referenced by artifacts[].url on the station and fetched by the Handler, or — in poll mode — uploaded by the station with PUT /visits/{visitId}/artifacts/{name}, where the Handler MUST verify the digest against the envelope and answer 409 on a mismatch.

11. Outcomes, cancellation, retries, revocation

Every visit ends in exactly one terminal outcome, and the words are part of the contract (ADR-026): a run’s completeness statement reports them to a data consumer verbatim, and implementations MUST keep the vocabulary exact in code, logs and user interfaces.

Outcome Where Meaning Whose word
Delivered PEP 3 The result passed inspection and left; inspection.outcome is passed or redacted. —​
Refused NEG Access was declined by the controller’s standing conditions, or by a person asked to decide. A controller’s decision, with their stated reason, carried verbatim.
Rejected PEP 1, PEP 2, PEP 3 A checkpoint’s verdict on a document or a result: an invalid descriptor, a payload that is not the one offered, an agreement that is not this visit’s, a result a duty forbids. A checkpoint’s; nobody decided anything.
TimedOut NEG or EXEC The approval wait ran out, or the Handler cancelled the visit. Nobody decided.
Failed EXEC — or NEG, when a station broke carrying out a decision already taken Machinery broke. —​
Revoked PEP 2 or EXEC The controller took the agreement out of force while the visit was queued or running; the station stops at its next checkpoint. A controller’s act over an agreement they granted, with their reason.
Skipped never reaches a station The run did not ask this station: a phase condition excluded it (NotSelected), the first phase did not deliver (NotEvaluated), it was reachable only through a missing visit (Pruned), or the failure policy dropped it (Dropped). The Handler’s record. No station can report it about itself.

Reporting a controller’s refusal as a rejection tells a consumer that their request failed a check when a person declined it; reporting a timeout as either puts a verdict in the mouth of somebody who never gave one; reporting a station’s own failure as a refusal attributes to a controller a decision they did not take. Every non-success event MUST carry the reason the consumer will see, and the envelope MUST repeat it.

11.1. Cancellation

POST /visits/{visitId}/cancel withdraws a visit: on approval timeout, on the run’s FailRun reaction, on a person stopping the run, at the budget deadline, or on replanning. A queued or pending visit ends immediately and the station emits negotiation.withdrawn; a running visit stops at its next checkpoint and the station emits visit.cancelled; either ends TimedOut. A visit already in a terminal state is unaffected, and the station answers 200 with its current status.

11.2. Revocation

A controller may take an agreement out of force while a visit under it is queued or running. The station MUST stop at its next checkpoint and emit visit.revoked, carrying the controller’s reason verbatim and naming the agreement, because a Handler applying the run’s onRevoked reaction needs it: outputs already derived from a revoked visit fall under that agreement’s duties. A visit that arrives afterwards presenting a revoked agreement was never running and is Refused at PEP 2, not Revoked; reporting it as Revoked would fire the run’s onRevoked over a visit that never began.

The presentation records what a component saw of the credential a visit was dispatched under, verbatim — because a component that normalised every form into "the agent" would write down something nobody said, and which form was presented is often what an audit is about. 12.5. VisitStatus and Problem

A VisitStatus is the station’s answer to a push, a replay, a cancellation, a signature and GET /visits/{visitId}: visit ✱, attempt, state ✱, agreement, sequence ✱ (of the latest event), approval (controller, expiresAt), progress, events and result URLs, and reason for a non-success outcome.

A Problem is an RFC 9457 problem details object [RFC9457] — type ✱, title ✱, status ✱, detail, instance — extended with the FDT outcome (Refused, Rejected or Failed), the checkpoint, and the justification. A station answering with a Problem MUST put the same outcome word in it that the visit’s terminal event carries.

12.6. Signature messages

An AgreementSignature (posted by the Handler to a push-dispatch station) and a PartySignature (carried in both directions of the poll-dispatch exchange) are one construction: signedBy ✱, onBehalfOf (present exactly when it differs from signedBy), keyId ✱, algorithm ✱ (EdDSA), signedDigest ✱ (sha256: + 64 hex), value ✱ (base64url, unpadded). 15. Security

15.1. Credentials

Version 1 uses OpenID Connect bearer tokens [OPENID-CONNECT-CORE]. A station MUST verify a visit’s token against the identity providers it trusts for the visit’s network (ADR-017), and MUST answer 401 — not an outcome word — when the token is missing or untrusted, since nothing has been decided about a caller whose identity was not established.

The Handler visits under its own credential (ADR-044). Nothing forwards a consumer’s credential to a station: a token minted for a Handler’s audience and presented to a station would be a confused deputy. Who dispatched the run reaches the station two ways, and the station records which it got:

  • the descriptor’s consumer.presentation carries the credential presentation of § 12.4 Presentation verbatim, and is always there;

  • where the network’s identity provider performs RFC 8693 token exchange [RFC8693], the descriptor also carries consumer.exchangedToken, which the station verifies against the network’s trusted issuer and which ties the visit cryptographically to the person named. An exchanged token establishes which person dispatched the run — the token’s subject and actor — and nothing else; a station MUST NOT treat any further claim in it as evidence in the negotiation and MUST NOT record one.

A station MUST record which of the two it got in every event’s requester.assurance —​presented-and-verified, with the exchange it established, or presented-only. Grading is the station’s act and nobody else’s: a descriptor whose presentation carries assurance or exchange MUST be rejected, because a Handler writing verified about a credential presented to it would be certifying its own assertion. A network without exchange is recorded, not broken, and a console MUST render the two states as two states.

Callbacks use the per-visit capability token in the descriptor. A poll-dispatch station authenticates at the Handler with the OIDC client credentials of its operator, trusted by the Handler for the network.

15.2. Transport and integrity

All traffic between the parties MUST be over TLS, and the schemas say so wherever a location is written down: handOver.url and artifacts[].url are https only. Integrity is by sha256: digests end to end — the train’s offer, the descriptor, the arrival check, the agreement’s three pins, the released artefacts and the hand-over. Signing keys are published as JWK sets by their owners and distributed out of band (§ 7.4.4 Refusing a signature).

Version 2 is planned to replace bearer tokens with credential presentations and to bind negotiation to the Dataspace Protocol (§ 16 Relationship to the Dataspace Protocol).

16. Relationship to the Dataspace Protocol

This section is non-normative.

The agreement and visit lifecycles map onto the Dataspace Protocol’s Contract Negotiation and Transfer Process [DSP]; in version 1 the mapping is documentation and a version 2 binding would implement it (ADR-019). The mapping is lossy in one place and the loss must not survive the crossing: DSP ends badly in exactly one way — TERMINATED with a reason — while this protocol distinguishes Refused, Rejected, TimedOut, Failed and Revoked and keeps Skipped outside the protocol altogether. A binding carries this protocol’s outcome word in every TERMINATED reason, verbatim; a binding that emits TERMINATED without it has implemented the part of the mapping that DSP could express.

Agreement state DSP Contract Negotiation
requested REQUESTED
matched OFFERED, or straight to AGREED (there are no counter-offers)
pending approval a provider-side wait before AGREED; DSP has no state for a person deciding
awaiting signature AGREED — the provider has sent the agreement and the consumer has not yet verified
active VERIFIED → FINALIZED
never concluded TERMINATED, carrying Refused, TimedOut or Rejected as the case is
suspended, revoked, expired, fulfilled after FINALIZED; DSP 2025-1 does not model them, and revocation after FINALIZED is a gap to raise
Visit state DSP Transfer Process
Queued, Checking, PendingApproval REQUESTED (SUSPENDED if already started)
Running, Inspecting STARTED
Delivered COMPLETED
Refused, Rejected, TimedOut, Failed, Revoked TERMINATED, reason carrying the word
Skipped no Transfer Process is ever created: nothing was requested of this station

Not covered by DSP and therefore this protocol’s own: progress and compliance events, checkpoint decisions and justifications, pending approval, onward-state hand-over and provenance.

17. Open points

This section is non-normative.

The following are recorded in the reference implementation’s question register with a default every implementation follows until the question is decided.

  • Q27 — what outcome a station records when the assignee never signs. Default: none; the visit rests until withdrawn (§ 7.4.5 When the assignee does not sign).

  • Q61 — whether a network’s trust anchors may vouch for a station’s signing key, instead of out-of-band configuration. Default: configuration; UnknownKey otherwise.

  • Q114 — whether onward state needs a permission of its own or travels on the permission for the action that produced it; an agreement with no fdt-p:deliverResult permission admits nothing and constrains nothing, and PEP 3’s class check then passes vacuously.

  • Q127 — whether justification.decision describes what the rules said or what happened to the visit (§ 12.2 Visit event).

  • Q130 / Q133 — whether a station that cannot verify a hand-over’s bytes must refuse at PEP 1 or may run and report the result unverifiable.

  • Q135 — a hop with two inputs: one handOver per descriptor today; precededBy can name both.

  • Q136 — who may fetch a hand-over from the station that holds it, under which credential, and how long the artefact lives.

  • Draft 2 — a JSON-LD context for descriptors and events; the exact SSE framing; artefact size limits and resumable downloads; a self-description vocabulary for dispatch modes and protocol version; station-to-station forwarding; the DSP binding.
